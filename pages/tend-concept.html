Phase 1 · Product discovery and visual design · working name "Tend"

A routine that fits your mouth, and is short enough to keep.

Most people leave the dental chair with generic advice and a pamphlet. Tend turns what is true about your teeth, your restorations and your health into the few daily actions that matter most for you, explains each one, and gets out of your way.

The recommendation

Build a rules-based routine coach, not an AI dentist and not a tracker.

My short answer after working through the problem. The three bets below are what I would defend, and the rest of this page shows the evidence for them.

1. The product is the subtraction

Dental advice is long. A person with an implant, mild gum bleeding and a dry mouth gets about twelve "shoulds". Tend's value is picking the three that matter most this month and sequencing them into a morning and evening routine under ten minutes.

2. Rules, with a human owner for every one

A versioned, readable rule set, each rule signed off by a named dentist, with a source and an evidence grade. It is testable, explainable on screen and safe to audit. A language model may reword explanations later. It never decides what you are told to do.

3. Honest about its limits

Tend does not diagnose. It has a hard stop for warning signs, it says "ask your dentist" when evidence is thin, and it measures success by consistency and self-reported gum comfort, not by a score that rewards doing more.

Wedge

Start with adults whose mouths changed: new implant, crown or bridge, braces or aligners, or a gum disease diagnosis.

These people have a reason to care now, a clinician who gave them instructions they forgot, and routines that really differ from the default. Healthy-mouth adults come second. Children are a separate product because the rules, the buyer and the consent model are all different.

Problem space

The gap is translation and follow-through, not knowledge.

Brushing twice a day is common knowledge. What people lack is the specific "how" for their own mouth, a reason to believe it, and a way to keep it up after the first fortnight.

Assumptions I would challenge

  • "Personalized" is mostly "different tools and technique", not different frequency. Twice-daily brushing and daily interdental cleaning hold for almost everyone. The personalization is which tool, how, and what extra.
  • More tracking is not better. A checklist with ten items trains people to tick without doing. Cap the daily routine, and show only what changes the plan.
  • Gamification can backfire. Streak loss punishes the exact person who needs forgiveness. Track weeks, not unbroken days.
  • Camera-based plaque detection is tempting and risky. Consumer photos are unreliable, and false reassurance is the worst failure. Defer.
Alternatives explored

Three ways to build this, one I would choose.

A

Self-serve consumer coach

User answers questions and gets a routine. Fast to launch, broad reach. Weak on data quality: people misreport what is in their mouth, and nobody clinical stands behind the plan.

B

Dentist-prescribed companion

Clinic sets up the profile and Tend delivers it. Best accuracy and trust. Slow: you need clinic sales, integrations and support before a single user arrives.

Recommended

C. Self-serve first, dentist-confirmable

Anyone can start in five minutes. The profile can later be "confirmed by my dentist" via a shareable summary or code, which unlocks clinician-only rules such as higher-strength fluoride. Gets users now, builds toward B without committing to it.

Engine: rules vs. AI

Rules win for the core. The inputs are structured, the outputs must be reproducible and defensible, and a clinician must be able to read and sign each decision. An LLM is appropriate only for rewording an approved explanation at a reading level, or answering free-text questions with the approved rule set as its sole source, and both are phase 2.

Platform: mobile app vs. web

Reminders and a daily habit favor a native-feeling app. Reaching someone from a dentist's link favors the web. I suggest a PWA-style responsive web app for validation, wrapped natively once retention is proven.

The experience

Five minutes to a plan, two minutes a day to keep it.

The flow runs left to right: tell us about your mouth, pass a safety check, receive a short routine with reasons, then live with it. Scroll the strip. Every "Why" opens the rule behind it.

Design language

Calm, bright, a little confident. Closer to a good fitness app than to a clinic.

Dental software looks like a waiting room: teal, stock smiles, clip-art teeth. Tend leans on clean enamel whites, a single saturated blue for action, and a warm gum red reserved for things that need attention. No mascot, no photos of teeth.

Subtract first

Four steps, not twelve. Every extra item must beat "left out on purpose".

Always show the why

One tap from any recommendation to its reason, evidence grade and reviewer.

Forgive lapses

Weeks and partial credit. Return after a gap with a welcome, not a broken streak.

Accessible by default

Large tap targets, 4.5:1 contrast, no meaning by color alone, works one-handed and with wet hands.

Clinical intelligence

A readable rule set that a dentist can sign and an engineer can test.

Profile in, routine out, with a trace of which rules fired and why. Deterministic, so the same profile always yields the same plan.

1 Profile

Structured answers: age band, restorations, history, conditions, concerns.

2 Safety gate

Red-flag rules run first and can halt everything.

3 Rule match

Each rule declares conditions, action, slot, evidence and priority.

4 Resolve

Specific rules supersede general ones; conflicts such as rinse timing are settled by explicit constraints.

5 Budget

Rank by priority and trim to a time budget. Drops are listed, not hidden.

6 Explain

Every step carries its rule ID, text, source and reviewer.

Why not an LLM at the core

It can't be signed off line by line, answers vary between runs, and a confident wrong answer about your implant is a patient-safety event. Use language models for wording and Q&A only, constrained to the approved rule text.

What makes it maintainable

Rules as data in version control, a golden-profile test suite (every release re-runs 200 sample mouths), review expiry dates so stale advice fails loudly, and a clinician console to read and approve changes.

Initial development scope

Smallest thing that proves the routine is worth keeping.

Build first
  • Intake for adults: restorations, gum history, dry mouth, diabetes, cavity history
  • Safety gate with stop screens
  • Rule engine with about 40 reviewed rules, trace and explanations
  • Routine and Today with reminders
  • Weekly gum check-in
  • Dentist summary to share (PDF or link)
Next
  • Clinician console and dentist confirmation code
  • Guided brushing with profile-aware cues
  • Product guidance (tool sizes, fluoride levels) without affiliate bias
  • Smart toothbrush data import
  • LLM rewording and constrained Q&A
Avoid
  • Diagnosis, or reading photos for disease
  • Streaks and points that punish gaps
  • Selling products inside the plan
  • Whitening, charcoal, oil pulling advice
  • Children's profiles until consent and dosing rules are designed

How we know it works

Four-week plan adherence (3+ interdental nights a week), self-reported bleeding trend, share of users who book the dentist visit the app prompted, and the rate at which the safety gate stops someone who then sees a dentist. Not time in app.

Before we build

Risks, trade-offs and the decisions I need from you.

Risks
  • Clinical liability. Advice to people with implants and conditions. Mitigate with sign-off, expiry, and tight "ask your dentist" wording; get legal review.
  • Regulation. A tool that triages symptoms can edge into medical-device territory (FDA, UK MDR, EU MDR). Keep the gate narrow and non-diagnostic, and confirm early.
  • Privacy. Health data (HIPAA, GDPR special category). Store the minimum, keep the profile on device where possible.
  • Guideline variation. Countries disagree, for example on fluoride levels and floss evidence. Rules need a region tag.
  • Retention. Habit apps decay. The plan: short routine, forgiving tracking, dentist-visit loop.
